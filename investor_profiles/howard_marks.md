# Howard Marks — price, cycles and the limits of knowledge

Comparative profile, 2026-10-25. Marks articulates Oaktree's philosophy; investment-team decisions must not automatically be credited to him personally.

## Philosophy, sourcing and valuation

Superior results require better-than-consensus judgment, not merely being different. Second-level thinking asks what is already in the price, how other investors feel and how expectations could change. Risk is more than short-term volatility: permanent impairment, inadequate prospective compensation and investor behavior matter. Marks's 2014 *Dare to Be Great II* states: “Most great investments begin in discomfort.” Discomfort alone is not a sufficient buy criterion. [1–2]

Search disfavored credit, distress and periods when the supply of willing capital has collapsed. Security selection requires issuer analysis, collateral, priority, covenants, recovery scenarios and price. The macro contribution is usually recognizing an extreme in risk appetite or credit availability, not repeatedly forecasting GDP or interest rates. A safe asset can be a bad investment at the wrong price; a distressed asset can offer adequate compensation. “Taking the temperature” is a conditional assessment of the cycle, not a precise market-timing clock. [1–3]

## Construction, duration, exits and avoidance

Balance offense and defense according to the opportunity set. Distressed investing requires capital able to wait through restructurings, diversified enough to absorb unknowns, and liquidity appropriate to commitments. Marks's selling memo explicitly contrasts concentration's potential rewards with diversification against what the investor does not know; it supplies no scientifically exact position limit. [4]

Hold on the basis of prospective risk-adjusted return, not a desire to realize gains or avoid reporting losses. Sell when the thesis weakens, a superior opportunity appears, or portfolio constraints justify it. “Selling Out” rejects selling solely because price rose or fell. Avoid overconfident forecasts, automatic profit-taking, uncritical extrapolation and ignoring where the market stands in its cycle. Public equity examples in memos are often illustrations, not disclosures of Oaktree trades. [3–5]

## Famous decisions / calls and stated reasoning

These are documented **allocation or risk-posture calls**, not asserted individual-stock purchases:

| Call | Rationale and source timing |
|---|---|
| Technology bubble warning, January 2000 | Excessive optimism and speculative pricing; *bubble.com* is the contemporaneous memo, reviewed in 2023. [3] |
| Increased caution, 2005–07 | Easy money, weak lending standards and insufficient risk premiums warranted defensive posture; 2007 *It's All Good* is the contemporary anchor. [3] |

| Investing amid pandemic fear, March 2020 | Decisions still had to be made under uncertainty; extreme prices could compensate for severe adverse scenarios. His 2023 account reviews the call, not proof of medical forecasting skill. [3] |

## Mistakes and what changed

*Something of Value* records a meaningful revision prompted by discussion with his son Andrew: rigidly dividing “value” from “growth,” using low multiples as a definition of value, and selling durable growers at a fixed target can be mistaken. Marks recognizes that a business's future prospects must enter intrinsic value. That is an evolution in analytical framing, not evidence he abandoned price discipline or that Oaktree became a technology-growth fund. [5]

His 2023 retrospective selects a few notable calls; it is not a full out-of-sample forecasting audit. Do not infer a perfect timing record. The memos also distinguish prudent recognition of extremes from the largely futile ambition to forecast ordinary short-term market moves. No specific named-security loss with a verified resulting portfolio-rule change is established in this source set; leave that gap explicit rather than substitute Amazon illustrations for personal holdings. [3–5]

He also calls his 2004 defensive warning “way-too-early” in the 2023 review. Recognizing excessive risk pricing and precisely timing its correction are different skills; posture changes must survive the wait. That is an admitted timing limitation, not evidence the later crisis was predictable to the month. [3]

## How an agent must be prompted differently

**Proposed instruction:** “State what can and cannot be known. Assess the price and capital-market temperature; distinguish issuer risk from security price. Examine default/recovery scenarios and adjust offense versus defense only when evidence is strong. Judge a sale on forward opportunity cost, not price history.” Relative to Ackman, emphasize credit rights and cyclical pricing of risk rather than managerial intervention; relative to Druckenmiller, do not require a high-frequency or precise macro forecast. [1–6; prompt is synthesis]
