# Warren Buffett — business ownership, price discipline, durable financing

Comparative profile, 2026-10-25. Historical philosophy, not a current portfolio recommendation. Later explanations below are hindsight unless explicitly dated contemporaneous.

## Philosophy, sourcing and valuation

“It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.” This 1989 formulation describes the evolution beyond Graham-style statistical bargains, not abandonment of margin of safety. Buffett asks whether a business is understandable, has durable economics, trustworthy management and an attractive price. The 1996 letter insists that knowing the boundaries of competence matters more than enlarging them. [1–2]

Sources include annual reports, long industry observation, discussions with managers and negotiated acquisitions. His 1951 GEICO account demonstrates direct investigation of insurance economics rather than buying because Graham owned it. Estimate distributable owner earnings and their durability; adjust reported earnings for maintenance capital and working capital. Treat growth as valuable only when incremental investment earns adequate returns. Berkshire's mix of controlled businesses, listed equities and insurance float is a structural advantage that an ordinary fund cannot simply assume. [3–5]

## Construction, duration, exits and avoidance

Concentrate when business understanding and price justify it; the 1993 letter rejects diversification as a requirement for the knowledgeable investor but does not prescribe concentration for everyone. Berkshire's liquidity commitments and insurance obligations coexist with concentrated listed holdings. “Our favorite holding period is forever” applies to outstanding businesses with outstanding management, not every security. Sell when economics or management deteriorate, valuation/opportunity cost changes, or capital allocation demands it; the Tesco discussion shows that inertia can still delay an exit. Avoid businesses whose future cannot be estimated, excessive debt, poor capital economics and paying growth prices for uncertain advantages. [2,5–8]

## Decisions and stated reasoning

| Decision | Underwriting rationale and source status |
|---|---|
| GEICO research, 1951; later major Berkshire ownership | Direct distribution, low expense structure and the economics of insuring selected drivers; the 1951 article is contemporary evidence. Do not merge his personal 1951 purchase with Berkshire's later control transaction. [3] |
| See's Candies, 1972 | Brand/pricing power and modest incremental capital needs; Buffett's 2007 account is retrospective and emphasizes cash generated beyond reinvestment. [5] |
| Coca-Cola purchases, 1988 | The 1988 letter places it among large holdings and explains the preference for outstanding businesses and long holding periods; the 1996 “Inevitables” discussion supplies later detail on brand and global demand. Do not backdate the later wording. [2,7] |
| BNSF agreement, 2009 | A long-lived transport franchise and confidence in the US economy, with substantial continuing capital needs; not a See's-like asset-light business. Buffett's 2014 review calls it an “all-in wager” on America's future. [9] |

## Mistakes and evolution

Dexter Shoe's apparent advantage proved illusory under foreign competition; paying in Berkshire stock magnified the opportunity cost. Buffett's 2007 letter distinguishes poor economics from the even greater mistake of issuing valuable shares. Tesco, discussed in the 2014 letter, illustrates delayed reaction to deteriorating management and operating evidence. The lesson is not “never sell” but preserve independent re-underwriting. His 2020 letter acknowledges paying too much for Precision Castparts because he misjudged normalized earning power; quality labels do not excuse valuation errors. These are specific admissions, not a claim that each produced a new rigid rule. [5,9–10]

## How an agent must be prompted differently

**Proposed instruction:** “Underwrite a business as an owner. Identify competence boundaries, moat durability, management incentives, owner earnings and incremental returns on capital. Give a price range and opportunity cost; decline rather than invent a forecast. Assume no activist intervention is necessary. Distinguish Berkshire's permanent capital/float from the agent's actual funding. Explain what would invalidate a very long holding period.” Unlike Ackman, do not default to a public campaign or a specific installed CEO; unlike Graham, do not stop at asset discounts. [1–8; prompt is synthesis]
