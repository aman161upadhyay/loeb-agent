# Peter Lynch — observed businesses, classified stories, verified numbers

Comparative profile, 2026-10-25. Lynch's examples below are predominantly retrospective book accounts, not reconstructed entry-date trading files.

## Philosophy, sourcing and valuation

“Invest in what you know” is a search advantage, **not** permission to buy without research. Familiarity with products or a local store generates a lead; subsequent analysis must establish profits, balance-sheet strength, competitive position and what growth the price already assumes. *One Up on Wall Street* organizes businesses into slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds and asset plays. Different categories require different questions; a cyclical with a low peak-earnings P/E can be dangerous. [1–2]

Sources include consumers, workplaces, store visits, suppliers, company discussions and filings. Look for under-followed companies, unglamorous names and expandable concepts before broad institutional sponsorship. Test the story with sales, margins, debt, inventories, cash flow and unit economics. P/E relative to sustainable earnings growth is useful, but a mechanical PEG screen cannot validate the story or replace cyclicality analysis. Expansion needs reproducible economics and remaining geographic runway. [1–2]

## Construction, duration, exits and avoidance

Lynch's Magellan approach involved many holdings and active investigation, unlike Ackman's deliberately small set. The books' advice to individuals is to own only businesses they can follow; neither a mandatory concentrated portfolio nor a clone of Magellan follows from that. Allocate by category and opportunity, rather than declaring every stock a permanent compounder. Let a successful fast grower continue while the story and economics hold. [1–2]

Sell when the category-specific story fails, competitive/financial evidence deteriorates, growth runway disappears, or valuation becomes unjustifiable; do not use a price increase alone as a sell signal. He criticizes pulling the flowers while watering weeds. Avoid fashionable “whisper” stocks, diversifications into unrelated businesses, overleveraged balance sheets, unsupported next-big-thing claims and treating industry experience as proof of cheapness. The discipline is company-specific rather than macro forecasting. [1–2]

## Decisions and stated reasoning

| Example | Lynch's account of the reason |
|---|---|
| Hanes / L'eggs | Household observation of a successful product and supermarket distribution prompted research into Hanes; the consumer insight supplied a lead, subsequently tested against business economics. [1] |
| La Quinta | An understandable, repeatable lodging format with attractive expansion economics; Lynch investigated the operating model rather than infer value from a familiar hotel sign. [1] |
| Dunkin' Donuts | A simple, observable business whose product and growth model he could understand; contrasted with difficult technological prediction. [1] |
| Chrysler turnaround | Analyze survival, debt and recovery potential rather than treat depressed recent earnings as a permanent valuation denominator. A turnaround is not a fast grower with a different label. [1–2] |

## Mistakes and what changed

Lynch uses J. Bildner & Sons, a local specialty-food retailer, as a warning against assuming that liking the original establishment means it can expand successfully. The expansion failed to reproduce the original economics. This corrects the simplistic folk version of “buy what you know”: investigate management, financing and replicability before extrapolating. His discussion contrasts this error with more careful La Quinta research. [1,3]

His selling lessons also acknowledge the opportunity cost of exiting winners and relying on superficial signals such as insider transactions without context. A sale by one insider may reflect personal diversification, not a broken company. The published books document analytical lessons; they do not support attributing an exact, permanent numerical stop-loss or universal portfolio weight to him. [1–2]

## How an agent must be prompted differently

**Proposed instruction:** “Begin with observed customer and operating evidence, then classify the business. State a short testable story. Verify the balance sheet and growth economics; explain why observed demand converts into per-share earnings. Specify category-specific milestones and sell rules. Search beyond large caps and do not require a governance catalyst.” Compared with Ackman, allow many smaller, unglamorous, fast-growing or cyclical opportunities; compared with a product-recommender, reject the stock unless price and financial evidence support it. [1–2; prompt is synthesis]
