# What makes a great investor?

Research synthesis; prepared 2026-10-25. This is a **process vocabulary, not an instruction to make Ackman imitate every successful investor**. Practitioner prescriptions are hypotheses and self-reports; academic findings are sample-dependent estimates. Neither a famous winner nor a profitable trade establishes skill. Citations identify publication/statement dates; retrospective descriptions must not enter earlier trade packets.

## 1. Temperament is useful only when disciplined by evidence

Buffett's circle of competence is a boundary on underwriting, not a claim to superior intelligence: “The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital.” Independent judgment, patience, willingness to do nothing, and capital that can survive adverse marks make an analytical advantage usable. They do not make stubbornness an edge. Buffett's distinction between price and business value supplies a reason to tolerate volatility; a deteriorating business is a different problem. Munger adds incentive analysis and psychological self-suspicion, including the possibility that multiple biases reinforce one another. [1–3]

Academic evidence is narrower than investor folklore. Barber and Odean associate high individual-investor turnover with poor net performance; this does not establish that all active trading is irrational or that professional macro trading cannot work. Kahneman and Klein argue that reliable intuition requires a sufficiently regular environment and opportunities to learn from feedback. Markets' noisy, delayed feedback makes unchecked confidence particularly dangerous. **Agent implication:** separate independence of thought from willingness to update; write disconfirmation tests before interpreting adverse prices. [4–5]

## 2. Competence, variant perception, base rates, inversion

**Circle of competence:** identify what must be forecast, what evidence supports that forecast, and where the analysis becomes unknowable. A recognizable brand is not sufficient. **Variant perception:** Steinhardt's formulation requires a well-founded view different from consensus, not merely an unpopular security. Ask: What does the price imply? What do other investors believe? What specific evidence makes our forecast different? What changes the market's mind, or allows cash realization without its agreement? Mauboussin reproduces Steinhardt's definition and emphasizes expectations rather than standalone “good company” analysis. [1,6]

**Base rates:** begin with outcomes for an economically relevant reference class—turnarounds, leveraged rollups, restaurant remediation, activist CEO replacements—then adjust for company-specific evidence. Do not select the reference class after observing the result. **Inversion:** ask how permanent loss occurs, including refinancing failure, incentives, regulatory intervention, technological substitution and the investor's own liquidity constraints. Munger's multidisciplinary approach complements, rather than replaces, arithmetic. Mauboussin's outside-view framework makes this operational; base rates are starting distributions, not mechanical vetoes. [2,7]

## 3. Margin of safety and quality are complementary, not synonyms

Graham's margin of safety protects against valuation error and adverse developments through price, balance-sheet support, earnings coverage and diversification. A low multiple is not automatically a margin of safety if normalized earnings are fictional. Buffett and Munger extend the framework toward businesses that reinvest at attractive returns, have durable competitive advantages and need little incremental capital. A fair price for durable economics may be safer than a large apparent discount to a wasting asset; an excellent business can still be overpriced. [8–10]

Long-duration compounding depends on **incremental** returns, reinvestment runway, competitive persistence, management and purchase price. “Quality” should therefore be decomposed into profitability, cash conversion, balance-sheet resilience, accounting reliability and durability. Frazzini, Kabiller and Pedersen find that cheap, safe, quality exposures and financing help explain Berkshire's record; their leverage estimates vary across paper versions. This is an explanatory factor decomposition, not proof that buying a quality screen recreates Buffett's judgment or Berkshire's financing. [11]

## 4. Concentration versus diversification: skill does not eliminate uncertainty

Cohen, Polk and Silli's original *Best Ideas* working paper identifies managers' high-conviction positions using portfolio-weight proxies and finds outperformance relative to other holdings and benchmarks. The later Antón/Cohen/Polk revision has different authorship, specifications and return estimates; do not mix its headline numbers with the original. Conviction is inferred, not observed. The result is evidence of stock-selection information, **not** a randomized demonstration that each fund should concentrate more or that its best ex-post winner was identifiable ex ante. [12]

Cremers and Petajisto define active share as one-half the sum of absolute portfolio-minus-benchmark weights. Their 1980–2003 mutual-fund sample associates high active share with better performance. Active share measures difference, not correctness: concentrated sector exposure, small-cap exposure and a genuinely differentiated stock-selection portfolio can have different risks. Frazzini, Friedman and Pomorski find that benchmark differences explain much of its predictive claim. Thus high active share can diagnose closet indexing but is not a standalone skill score. [13–14]

Diversification reduces idiosyncratic exposure but not shared economic shocks. Concentration increases the payoff to genuine insight and the damage from model error. Bessembinder's evidence that a small minority of stocks account for aggregate net wealth creation also warns that narrow portfolios can miss exceptional winners. **Agent implication:** show security, industry, factor, funding and common-thesis exposures; never infer optimal concentration from a celebrated investor's surviving portfolio. [15]

## 5. Position sizing: Kelly is a discipline, not a license

Kelly maximizes expected logarithmic wealth under specified probabilities and payoffs. For a binary bet with net odds \(b\), win probability \(p\), and loss probability \(q=1-p\), the unconstrained fraction is \(f^*=(bp-q)/b\). This formula is not a stock portfolio optimizer: equity outcomes are continuous, correlated, time-varying and poorly known. Thorp discusses the criterion in gambling and investment, including fractional betting and practical risks. A positive expected return does not imply a tolerable drawdown or adequate liquidity. [16–17]

**Proposed agent protocol:** estimate downside/upside distributions and correlation; haircut estimated edge for uncertainty; cap exposure by permanent-loss budget, liquidity and institutional constraints; stress funding and gap moves; use fractional-Kelly intuition only if inputs can be defended. A concentrated investor with permanent capital and a levered investor facing redemptions should not receive identical sizes. These are implementation recommendations, not a claim that Ackman or every profiled investor literally runs Kelly.

## 6. Process versus outcome; journals and checklists

Mauboussin distinguishes skill and luck and emphasizes sample size and repeatable process. Annie Duke calls judging decisions solely by realized outcomes “resulting.” A sound probabilistic decision can lose; a reckless bet can win. Conversely, repeatedly invoking bad luck can immunize a defective model. Grade evidence quality, calibration, alternatives and observance of precommitted rules independently from realized P&L, then use outcomes to update the model over many decisions. [18–19]

Spier's *The Education of a Value Investor* describes environmental safeguards and an investment checklist; Pabrai and Spier appear in Gawande's discussion of checklists in investing. The influence runs from aviation/medicine's prevention of omission toward investment error prevention—not from randomized evidence that an investing checklist creates alpha. A checklist should be short enough to use and adapted from actual errors; it cannot substitute for valuation. A **decision journal** additionally preserves information available before the result, reducing retrospective rewriting. [20–21]

**Suggested pretrade record:** dated evidence; consensus and variant forecast; probability-weighted valuation; relevant base rate; three ways to lose; competing explanations; size and funding limits; expected milestones; falsifiers; sell/trim conditions; reviewer disagreement. Preserve the original and append updates rather than overwrite it. Review rejected ideas as well as purchases to detect omission and selection bias. This is a proposed agent design, not a universal historical practice attributed to all investors.

## 7. Activism: creating a path to value realization

Operational activism changes management, productivity, asset deployment or strategy; financial activism changes payout, leverage, ownership structure or asset monetization. They overlap, but a buyback funded by debt is not the same mechanism as improved railway operations. Brav, Jiang, Partnoy and Thomas report positive announcement effects and changes in operating/payout measures in their early hedge-fund activism sample. Brav, Jiang and Kim's plant-level study finds productivity improvements over three years, stronger in business-strategy interventions, and improvements at sold plants under new owners. Labor outcomes matter: productivity gains should not be conflated with benefits for every stakeholder. [22–23]

These studies support activism as a plausible information-and-intervention edge, not a guarantee of activist skill or full causal identification. Target selection, anticipation, sale of the company, and heterogeneous campaign objectives complicate interpretation. Announcement returns measure the market's revision in expectations, not realized fund alpha net of fees, costs and failed campaigns. **Ackman-specific test:** identify the executable change, authority needed, coalition, CEO/board candidate, time and cost. Value the business if the campaign fails; do not assume the market adopts the presentation. [22–23]

## 8. Selling deserves independent research

Akepanidtaworn, Di Mascio, Imas and Schmidt examine institutional portfolios and find evidence of buying skill but selling decisions that underperform random-sale counterfactuals. Sales disproportionately select positions with salient extreme prior returns, consistent with asymmetric attention. Their counterfactual design is not a universal rule to sell randomly, nor evidence that every sale is bad; information-related selling behaves differently. The paper appeared as NBER WP 29076 in July 2021 and in *Journal of Finance* in 2023. [24]

Marks argues against selling merely because a stock rose or fell. More defensible reasons are a weakened thesis, excessive valuation relative to prospective return, superior alternatives, or portfolio risk constraints. **Agent implication:** write a fresh forward-return memorandum for sales, explicitly excluding purchase price, embarrassment and desire to fund a new exciting idea as sufficient reasons. Distinguish thesis invalidation from valuation realization; require the same research standard as a purchase. [25]

## 9. Failure modes—even for accomplished investors

| Failure | Diagnostic | Proposed control |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis creep | Original cash-flow claim becomes “assets,” then “new management,” then “recovery” without re-underwriting | Retain original thesis; each new thesis requires a new investment decision |
| Sunk cost / averaging down | Add because a large loss must be recovered, not because expected return improved | Ask whether the position would be initiated at today's price with no ownership |
| Public commitment escalation | Campaign success or personal vindication displaces investment return | Independent reviewer; separate advocacy budget from capital risk |
| False precision | Narrow DCF range conceals uncertain economics | Scenario distributions, base rates, explicit unknowns |
| Liquidity/funding mismatch | Correct long-run economics but forced exit | Stress collateral, redemption and refinancing paths |
| Correlated conviction | Several “different” holdings depend on one financing or consumer regime | Aggregate common drivers, not just tickers |

The mechanisms are consistent with experimental work on escalation of commitment and sunk costs, but applying them to an individual investor is an analytical hypothesis, not a psychological diagnosis. Herbalife's costly, public short campaign and Valeant's business/management problems make useful **tests** of thesis drift and commitment, not proof that those biases caused every decision. Ackman's later interviews and Pershing's reports describe failures and changes toward simpler, more predictable businesses; label those explanations hindsight. For replay, contemporaneous materials control. [26–28]

## 10. What research can and cannot call “skill”

Look for repeatable informational advantage, calibrated forecasts, risk-adjusted performance net of implementable costs, persistence across appropriate samples, and a mechanism that survives competition and capacity limits. Best-ideas evidence supports selective stock-picking information; active-share evidence is disputed; selling research exposes a process weakness even among institutions; activism evidence identifies potential channels of value creation. None establishes a universal great-investor personality or a universal number of holdings. Survivor selection and self-attribution contaminate practitioner accounts. The useful common denominator is **evidence-based, price-sensitive judgment plus survival and updating**, implemented differently by mandate. [5,12–15,22–24]
