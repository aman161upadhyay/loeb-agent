The automotive sales benchmark is an industry-demand measure, not a direct predictor of this supplier's receipts. The GM/non-GM split shows that distress is broader than one customer; it does not make customer concentration immaterial.

The comparison set is reorganizing auto suppliers and secured industrial claims, not debt-free global component manufacturers. Customer concentration, labor obligations, geographic mix and plant costs determine post-restructuring earning power. A normalized industry-volume assumption needs a bridge from current cash burn to sustainable margins. The pack does not assume an auto recovery already occurred or that every supplier receives the same government support. [1,2]

## Economic cross-check

Delphi supplies automotive components and systems and is operating in Chapter 11. Its manufacturing footprint, customer programs and engineering relationships can have going-concern value despite an impaired old capital structure. GM is a critical customer and restructuring counterparty. Old common stock, prepetition debt and debtor-in-possession claims are different securities; the existence of a valuable industrial business does not establish a recovery for every layer. [1,2]

GM dependence creates correlated customer and financing risk. Production cuts can intensify cash burn, and the sale process may assign valuable assets to other parties while leaving obligations behind. Seniority does not eliminate litigation, collateral disputes, new-money requirements or time risk. Buying the wrong claim can produce a poor recovery even if the company survives. The financing hearing is a catalyst but can also reveal dilution or priority changes. [1,2; risk analysis]

## Underwriting interpretation

Comparison discipline: align the unit being valued before applying a peer benchmark. Revenue recognition, geographic exposure, product mix, capital intensity, legal seniority and ownership of assets can all make a superficially similar multiple inappropriate. Use a segment or claim-level comparison when the group combines distinct economics. Higher margins elsewhere may identify a question worth asking; they do not prove the entire difference can be eliminated.

A credible normalization case identifies which costs change, what investment is required and whether the company has the ability to sustain the proposed earnings. Cyclical peaks should not be capitalized as perpetual growth. Equally, depressed earnings can understate an asset's potential if a specific operating or legal reset is under way. The benchmark should describe the same accounting measure and period, not a convenient headline from another date. Missing numerical comparables remain missing rather than being filled from a later investor presentation. Competitive response and transaction costs belong in the comparison.
