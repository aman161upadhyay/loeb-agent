Title: Potlatch Corporation — Business, assets and economic claim
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/79716/000103221003000433/ddef14a.htm; https://www.lmtribune.com/northwest/potlatchs-voting-may-be-an-issue-5c6f945f; internal desk state where labeled
Date: 2003-05-01

Potlatch combines forest-product manufacturing with timber-related assets. Its consumer-products operations include paper towels, napkins and facial and bathroom tissue. The company is building a $66 million Las Vegas towel plant while product pricing suffers from excess supply. Management argues the investment reflects long-term industry cycles rather than the present downturn. Valuing forest assets separately from loss-making manufacturing is an analytical possibility, not an announced transaction. [2]

## Economic cross-check

The proxy reports 2002 loss per share of $8.23 and negative 33.1% return on common equity, versus negative 4.1% for a sample of 15 forest-products peers. CEO Siegel's reported base salary was $578,880 and his annual grant covered 66,000 options, with a $475,200 grant-date model value. No cash management-performance bonus was paid for 2002 because earnings failed the plan minimum. [1]

The company's own proxy supplies a 15-company forest-products ROE comparison: a materially worse loss than the industry despite sector weakness. Differences in product mix, asset impairments and capital intensity must be reconciled before attributing the entire gap to stewardship. Tissue pricing is affected by oversupply; the proposed plant is intended to make stronger, more absorbent towels rather than undifferentiated capacity alone. [1,2]

## Underwriting interpretation

Analyst distinctions, not additional reported facts: identify which assets actually generate cash, which require reinvestment, and which can be transferred independently. Contractual ownership, economic exposure and voting control need not coincide. A recognizable product or attractive asset is not sufficient evidence of attractive common-equity economics. Separate a going-concern franchise from its financing before assigning a floor. Consider what remains if the proposed event does not occur: recurring cash, realizable assets, enforceable priority or only optionality. These distinctions prevent the business description from silently turning into a recommendation.

The observable tests are customer or counterparty retention, unit profitability, required maintenance investment and the ability to fund obligations without diluting the claim being evaluated. Where consolidated reporting combines unlike activities, a segment bridge is needed before comparing margins. Missing segment or legal information is not equivalent to a zero liability. No assumption about a future transaction, governance concession or recovery is embedded in this description.
